Local Specialized Freight Trucking in Canada
About This Industry
Definition Canadian local specialized freight trucking companies provide local trucking services using specialized equipment within a metropolitan area. Companies transport bulk liquids, dry bulk materials, forest products, automobiles, boats, livestock and other specialized freight. This does not include used goods transportation.

Executive Summary Long road ahead: Growth in the Canadian economy will fuel demand for specialized transportation services
Local specialized freight truckers in Canada have faced significant volatility amid wide swings in upstream production capacities since 2019. Surging industrial activity from mining companies and manufacturers supported revenue in the wake of the pandemic, but recent high interest rates have cut into demand from the construction sector. Skyrocketing oil prices supported demand for liquid bulk transportation as Canadian extractors produced more oil. Still, trucking companies continue to contend with a labour shortage that has limited their ability to fully service clients' needs. Revenue has been slipping at a CAGR of 0.3% to $11.1 billion through the current period, including an expected 0.9% dip in 2024 alone.
Fuel costs have played an especially prominent role through the current period, surging as supply chains ramped and sanctions on Russian oil weighed on the world's supply. Larger local specialized freight trucking companies successfully levied fuel surcharges or mitigated prices with forward contracts, but smaller competitors weren't able to impose the same additional costs. Some were forced out of the industry, exacerbating larger companies' dominance and lifting concentration. Still, lingering competition has prevented truckers from fully passing on fuel costs and preserving profit.
Moving forward, companies will recover as strong economic growth fuels demand for specialized transportation services. Many carriers will benefit from the increased use of just-in-time inventory management and rising supply chain integration. These trends will enable more frequent delivery and increase the complexity of clients' supply chains, encouraging large-scale corporations to outsource trucking operations to third-party truckers. The Bank of Canada's eventual lowering of interest rates will spur demand from construction companies, helping offset lackluster mining activity. Revenue is set to climb at a CAGR of 1.5% to $12.0 billion through the end of 2029.

What's driving current industry performance?
Fuel surcharges have supported revenue growth
•
The pandemic and the following recovery led to significant crude oil price volatility. Russian sanctions following the invasion of Ukraine further strained supply and exacerbated volatility since the former is an OPEC+ member and key oil exporter. Local specialized freight truckers have imposed fuel surcharges to offset rising costs and preserve profit.
•
Rampant demand for consumer goods and construction materials boosted industrial production through 2021 and 2022. Manufacturers, mining companies and wholesalers were willing to pay heightened transportation costs as they were more likely to recoup them downstream, leading to the strongest growth in the period for specialized trucking enterprises.
•
Less pronounced negotiating power hindered smaller trucking companies and owner-operators' ability to levy fuel surcharges. Larger carriers could leverage strong reputations and speedier delivery to facilitate higher prices. Concentration has ticked upward as major local specialized truckers gained market share through fuel cost volatility. Still, the industry remains highly fragmented and there are no major companies present in the industry.
•
Recovering production worldwide helped bring down the price of crude oil in 2023, even as sanctions on Russian oil remain in place. Declining operating costs made room for price-based competition and led to an uptick in new entrants. These benefits are expected to taper off in 2024 as several OPEC+ members voluntarily cut oil production by 2.2 million barrels a day, slightly lifting prices.
Trucking companies struggle to fill vacant positions
•
An aging workforce, long hours and pay concerns are causing high driver turnovers and pressuring the industry's ability to meet fluctuations in need for distribution. Operating conditions for truck drivers are exposing structural issues within the trucking industry, which continues to struggle to attract talent and subsequently effectively respond to fluctuations in downstream demand.
•
According to a recent Nanos Research survey of local freight truckers, driver shortages are the primary constraint on capacity. Many companies have raised wages to attract new workers. Trucking HR Canada reported in March 2024 that 57.0% of Canadian trucking companies couldn't fill all available positions in 2022. Still, this marks a drop from 67.0% in the previous year, indicating a slowly but steadily recovering job market.
•
Worker shortages are particularly challenging in high-demand areas like Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta. Alberta and Ontario are key players in Canada's natural resource and manufacturing output, so the shortage puts constraints on the supply chain. However, local trucking industries face fewer issues than long-haul trucking industries. Many local specialized freight truckers work within their communities and avoid the extended time away from home and other challenges faced by truckers in other sectors.

Recovering construction and manufacturing markets support growth
•
Many carriers transport heavy machinery, particularly for construction and manufacturing. These markets recovered as pandemic restrictions eased, aiding truckers' slow recovery and counteracting volatile oil prices.
•
Following the reopening, housing starts and nonresidential construction thrived. In contrast, Canada's automotive industry initially contracted as companies sought cheaper labor and production options. However, rising consumption boosted car and automobile manufacturing, contributing to growth in 2022 and 2023.
•
Local freight truckers have historically specialized in automobile transportation; this market's decline has forced many local carriers to shift to other specialties. This shift also represents a significant sunk cost for many truckers since car trailers are expensive. In response, companies have pushed into more stable segments, particularly forestry products and climate-controlled packed goods shipping.
High interest rates have brought a downturn in commodity prices
•
While soaring crude oil prices led to volatile fuel costs, they also spurred activity from oil and natural gas extraction companies, supporting the specialized truckers' revenue expansion seen through 2021 and 2022. Supply chain disruptions and high prices encouraged Canadian extractors to expand output rapidly. Local specialized freight truckers saw heightened freight volumes as more liquid bulk products were produced and more specialized equipment required transport.
•
The Bank of Canada imposed a series of interest rate hikes in 2023 to combat inflation. High interest rates make borrowing capital for new investments prohibitively expensive for many construction, mining and manufacturing industries, leading to a dip in the price of building materials like steel and lumber. Lower demand pushed upstream manufacturers to lower production and avoid perpetuating price drops, which dampened freight volumes for many truckers through 2023 and 2024.
•
The reduction of the policy rate in the second half of 2024 is spurring activity in the construction and industrial markets. Still, given the late implementation, the effects aren't expected to have a meaningful impact on performance through 2024, leaving the benefits of easing to be felt starting in 2025.
Trucking companies continue to invest in the EV transition
•
The transition to electric vehicles remains one of the major focus points for truckers. Regulators are continuing to push the trucking industry to curb emissions and help the country meet its net-zero pledges, in line with the global commitment to sustainability.
•
Electric vehicles are still a small part of commercial fleets, but the move towards electric trucks is gaining momentum. In 2023, electric truck sales surged by 67.0%, according to Geotab. While range and charging infrastructure are still major concerns, around 50.0% of current fleets, particularly in rural areas, could successfully transition to EVs due to their range compatibility.
•
The Canadian electric truck market is benefiting from higher weight limits on trucking. These limits allow carriers to load up to 92,000.0 pounds of freight, in contrast to the 80,000.0 pounds mandated by the US government. The higher weight limits benefit the transition to electric vehicles because EV trucks' batteries are inherently heavier than diesel engines, lowering the volume of cargo a vehicle can carry. Higher weight limits allow truckers to offset the more costly EV vehicles by transporting large loads, making the impact of transition less impactful on their financial performance.

Volatility Moderate
Crude oil volatility brings revenue swings
•
Dramatic shifts in the world price of crude oil following the pandemic caused diesel fuel prices to fluctuate wildly, raising overhead costs significantly. Profit faltered amid high fuel prices, but rising domestic production has recently reversed this trend.
•
Companies impose fuel surcharge fees to offset heightened operating costs. Local specialized freight truckers occupy a position as by far the most cost-effective option for carrying cargo short distances, so they can typically tack on these surcharges with little loss to demand.
•
When crude oil prices fall, local specialized freight truckers gain more room to lower prices. Smaller competitors with less expansive client lists undercut more expensive companies to gain market share, leading to downward pressure on revenue.
The industry's specialized nature mitigates volatility
•
Companies fill niches left open by other shipping and transportation industries, filling a wide range of liquid, dry and forestry product shipping lanes. Carriers will also transport automobiles, heavy machinery and other sensitive items.
•
Diversified revenue streams enable companies to withstand volatility. Even so, smaller companies may operate through a single niche, like liquid bulk transportation, since specialized truck costs are high.
•
Sweeping shifts in macroeconomic factors like consumer spending can push manufacturers and other buyers to cut their production capacities. Dampened production upstream translates to lower freight volumes and revenue for local specialized freight truckers.
Vendor-distributor business partnerships provide stability
•
The Canadian trucking industry relies more on relationships than the US, focusing on partnerships between distributors and vendors. A smaller spot market helps minimize supply and demand fluctuations, leading to less volatility.
•
A large portion of Canadian freight consists of raw materials, which reduces seasonality. Unlike the US, Canadian agricultural freight is less seasonal due to the dominance of grain in the distribution sector. Since grain can be stored, it helps stabilize distribution needs and spot rates.

Outlook  2024-29 Revenue CAGR +1.5%
Monetary easing will fuel Canadian economic recovery
•
The ongoing easing of monetary policy will support economic recovery, driving the industrial output expected to expand by a CAGR of 1.3% through 2029. Climbing factory activity will drive the need for specialized distribution, supporting a rebound in trucking services from a slowdown experienced through 2023 and 2024.
•
According to RBC, the real GDP growth in Canada is expected to accelerate from 1.1% in 2024 to 1.6% in 2025, supported by monetary easing. A rebound in economic activity will drive demand for trucking and local distribution contracts and support a recovery from the subdued performance recorded by specialized trucking companies through 2023 and 2024.
•
The softness in mining output and oil and gas extraction is expected to be offset by a forecast expansion in automobile and animal production, along with increasing value in nonresidential construction. As supply chains become more complex, clients are likely to continue outsourcing their transportation needs to local providers rather than starting in-house departments.
•
The rise of just-in-time (JIT) inventory management among manufacturers and retailers will boost trucking activity. JIT focuses on delivering smaller, more frequent shipments to cut inventory costs. However, its impact on specialized freight movements will be less significant than on general freight trucking due to the industry's non-standardized cargo transport.

Environmental oversight will likely ramp up through 2029 as the Canadian federal government prioritizes controlling emissions and developing infrastructure for electric trucks. Intensifying sustainability regulations will add another element of complexity for clients looking to transport their own goods.

Fleet owners will seek to acquire hybrid, battery and plug-in electric vehicles to advertise a more environmentally friendly fleet and eliminate fuel price uncertainty. Many downstream clients will emphasize sustainable supply chains, encouraging truckers to adopt electric trucks in the future.

Construction companies and food manufacturers will up freight volumes
•
Construction markets, including residential, nonresidential and utility construction, will benefit from improved financing conditions as the Bank of Canada continues to lower the policy interest rate. Contractors hire specialized truckers to move heavy machinery, like forklifts and excavators, alongside key construction inputs, like sand, gravel, cement and precast materials. Consequently, rising construction activity will boost contracts for logistics services, particularly for trucking companies offering first and last-mile delivery services.
•
The value of nonresidential construction, in particular, is expected to ramp up from a CAGR of 0.9% between 2019 and 2024 to a CAGR of 2.3% through 2029, providing a substantial growth boost from one of the core markets for specialized trucking.
•
Higher per capita disposable income will encourage consumers to trade up to fresher produce and livestock, which require climate-controlled transportation services. Climate-controlled transportation services make up nearly two-fifths of local specialized freight truckers' revenue, leaving many providers equipped to handle a rise in demand for distribution services.
The expected drop in extraction levels is set to be offset by improving downstream demand
•
The Canadian mining sector is set to contract as global supplies of coal and iron start to outstrip demand. Mining companies will cut production to preserve profit, leading to lower volumes of dry bulk goods for local specialized freight truckers to carry to their final destinations.
•
The completion of projects like the Trans Mountain Pipeline in 2024 will bolster fuel extraction. Still, the expected decline in fuel costs will pressure the industry, which is expected to contract by a CAGR of 1.0% through 2029, down substantially from the CAGR of 5.6% between 2019 and 2024, as fuel prices' surge caused producers worldwide to ramp up production.
•
A decline in fuel costs will directly benefit truckers by lowering operating expenses and boosting profits. Despite traveling shorter distances, local truckers had to absorb the cost spikes due to intense competition that prevented them from fully passing costs downstream in the form of surcharges. While local specialized trucking performs better than long-distance trucking when fuel prices rise, stable diesel prices will enhance the industry's profitability.
•
Stabilizing crude oil prices will create growth opportunities in downstream industries as falling distribution costs will trickle down to retail markets and support the distribution of crops, livestock, bulk liquids and dry bulk as aggregate demand ramps up, driving up contracts for transporting specialized freight.
Life Cycle Mature
Contribution to GDP
Industry Value Added (IVA) is set to lag behind CA GDP growth over the ten years through 2029 as local specialized freight truckers' contribution to the CA economy is outstripped by other trucking industries experiencing more robust growth.
Market Saturation
There are few suitable alternative methods of transporting perishable goods, waste and hazardous materials. Low barriers to entry enable new companies, especially owner-operators, to enter the market freely, which somewhat offsets closing businesses and keeps the market heavily saturated.
Innovation
Autonomous trucks and electric vehicles will slowly enter the industry and push companies to restructure their fleets. Autonomous vehicles will enable companies to slash wage expenses. Still, the adoption of fully autonomous fleets will take time and will have to face heavy regulatory scrutiny before it's deployed.

Consolidation
Medium to small trucking companies are regularly bought out, but contracts with nonemployers benefit both parties and will remain popular. Companies often consolidate to access new markets or services.
Technology and Systems
New technology focuses on improving route efficiency through computer systems that enable carriers to identify location, speed, downtime, weather and other data regarding each truck and its cargo.

Products and Services How are the industry’s products and services performing?
Volatile oil production has disrupted liquid bulk transportation
•
Local specialized freight truckers earn significant revenue by transporting petroleum products, chemicals and gases. Tankers and other trucks carrying flammable or toxic goods must display special warning emblems to alert police and firefighters of potential dangers in case of an accident.
•
Oil tankers are special cylindrical containers connected to trucks and serve as the primary method for distributing oil. On-the-road (OTR) transportation allows for scheduling shipments at any time and place, allowing merchants more flexible deliveries in comparison to pipeline and rail.
•
Liquid bulk transportation is facilitated by trucks in areas lacking pipeline infrastructure. Still, pipelines remain the primary distribution method for gas, oil and petroleum products. Economies of scale and the existing infrastructure allow pipelines to contract more distribution volumes than trucks, and in times of rising fuel costs, it tends to outperform the trucking distribution.
•
A war in Ukraine and other conflicts worldwide, especially in the Middle East, caused volatility in the energy markets, causing the price of diesel to skyrocket between 2019 and 2024. Rising distribution costs propelled vendors to shift distribution capacity to pipelines and limited their usage of trucks, pressuring the segment through 2024.
Dry bulk transportation swells alongside construction activity
•
Dry bulks include a wide variety of materials, including coal, grains, gravel, sand and iron ore. Truckers can load dry bulk freight onto dry bulk or pneumatic trailers.
•
Pneumatic trailers are vacuum-sealed tanks that use compressed air to prevent outside air from entering the tank, protecting the cargo from germs or moisture. Truckers often use pneumatic trailers to transport grains and powders, like cement.
•
Construction activity drives this segment as contractors purchase many dry bulk materials and finished goods from gravel and sand to iron. Strong construction activity has supported this segment but is expected to trend downward through the end of 2024 as tightened monetary policy pressures businesses' financing ability.
The rising operating costs and tariffs pressure demand from the forestry products transportation
•
Canada is a major lumber-producing country, generating a relatively stable demand for specialized freight truckers capable of transporting lumber, wood pulp and finished consumer products between lumber mills and other forestry-related facilities.
•
As reported by CBC, the US doubling the tariff duties on softwood lumber in August 2024, the lack of viable timber and high operating costs are pressuring the Canadian lumber industry and causing mill closures. The falling aggregate capacity of lumber pressures the distribution for specialized trucking despite Canada's leading position in wood pulp manufacturing. A decline in the paper industry exacerbates the impact, further reducing shipment volumes.
•
The forestry products transportation is continuing to decline relative to other segments, drifting lower from 9.4% of revenue in 2017 to an estimated 8.9% in 2024.

Flatbed trucks support the distribution of varying cargo. Rigs, pipes and drilling equipment can't fit in standard dry van trucks, and oftentimes, they have to be transported to remote locations without rail access, driving the need for specialized truck services.

Dry bulk carriers and pneumatic trailers support the mining and dry bulk companies
•
Mining companies and farmers use local specialized freight companies' dump trucks to transport coal, iron ore, corn, wheat and other dry bulk commodities to nearby processing facilities, hubs or wholesalers. Construction companies use specialized trucking services to transport construction materials, including gravel and cement, to construction sites.
•
Dry bulk transport is the largest market for specialized trucks. Trucking companies servicing this market operate dry bulk carriers and pneumatic trailers equipped with vacuums and compartments, allowing for seamless distribution of the commodities. The business depends on following procedures related to clearing the trucks and valves to avoid cross-contamination between shipments.
•
A strong expansion in mining and high demand from the construction sector fueled market growth. However, tightened monetary policy curbed growth in 2023 and is expected to lead to a broader decline in services through 2024.
Reefers' practicality supports the usage from the food and beverage manufacturers
•
Food and beverage manufacturing industries produce a wide range of temperature-sensitive goods, making local specialized freight truckers crucial to delivering preserved products. Food's essential role in sustaining the population makes this market especially lucrative for truckers.
•
Strong consumer spending has pushed food and beverage manufacturers to up their production capacities. As they've produced more goods, local specialized freight truckers have carried more loads and generated more revenue, leading to expansion in the market.
Commodity, petroleum and oil extraction companies create stable demand
•
Oil producers, refineries and chemical manufacturers hire tanker trucks to move oil, petroleum, petroleum derivatives and chemicals. These buyers also use specialized trucks to move heavy equipment and other oversized loads.
•
Tankers often carry higher risks than other specialized freights. Supply chain disruptions through the current period have limited many truckers' oil transportation volumes, though a heightened focus on domestic production has offset uncertainty worldwide.
•
Pipelines are the largest competitors for local truckers as many gas stations, petroleum refineries and other end destinations are connected to them. Specialized truckers tend to deliver the commodities in regions where pipeline infrastructure isn't viable or to smaller establishments lacking the capital for a direct connection to a pipeline network.
A lack of alternatives pushes livestock companies to continue relying on trucking for transport
•
The livestock market involves the buying and selling of animals, including cattle, pigs and poultry. Truckers providing services to this market operate livestock or gooseneck trailers, typically called bull haulers. The trailers are designed with secure gates, adequate airflow and partitions dividing up the animals during transport.
•
The impact of livestock distribution is relatively small on specialized freight trucking because of the extensive distances between farms and warehouses, making the operations fall outside of the scope of Local Specialized Freight Trucking in Canada.
•
New animal welfare and transport regulations in Canada have increased logistical complexity and documentation requirements. Regulatory bodies now prohibit transporting "unfit" animals and mandate detailed records of livestock transport hours. Additionally, animals must be delivered directly to slaughter within 12.0 hours.
•
The expansion of dry bulk outpaced animal production in Canada, causing the market to decline as a share of revenue despite the CAGR of 3.0% between 2019 and 2024.
Canada's declining automotive sector stifles demand
•
Car manufacturers and dealers rely on specialized freight trucking services to transport vehicles from factories to dealerships. Companies providing these automobile transportation services have specialized equipment, creating a niche market with higher entry barriers compared to other markets.
•

What impacts the industry’s market share concentration?
The industry’s fragmentation supports nonemployers and niche specializations
•
The industry has low barriers to entry, making it easy for small-scale businesses and nonemployers to enter the market. Nonemployers represent more than half of enterprises. This fragmentation makes it challenging for larger trucking companies to generate significant market share. Instead, most companies subcontract operations to local truckers.
•
The companies operate in local areas, often facilitating first and last-mile delivery services. While rising equipment costs can deter potential entrants, businesses often develop niches that allow them to curb startup costs and secure contracts after developing expertise in transporting a specific type of cargo.
Large carriers benefit from diversified operations
•
Small and medium-sized enterprises are the primary targets of buyouts as large companies expand operations and capture more of the market. More prominent companies will target smaller trucking companies to expand into new geographic markets or expand the scope of their value-added services.
•
Established fleet operators, particularly those with fleets operating across many trucking industries, including the truckload, less-than-truckload and general freight distribution, enjoy a more robust resource allocation in comparison to small enterprises.
•
The fragmented nature of the industry and many downstream markets requiring specialized trucking services prevents any company from establishing a pronounced market share, which is consistent with trends in other trucking industries.

Labour Expenses

What regulations impact the industry?
The National Safety Code
Local Specialized Freight Trucking in Canada
Provincial and local authorities carry out federally-set oversight under the Transportation Act Review. Canada's National Safety Code aims to enhance trucking safety, boost efficiency and create uniform safety standards across all provinces and territories. Regulations include single-driver licences, performance evaluations, examiner training, self-certification and roadside inspections.
The Transportation of Dangerous Goods Act
The Transportation of Dangerous Goods Act sets federal standards for transporting hazardous materials, which provincial governments adopt either fully or partially. Regulations cover classification, identification, routing and handling processes for dangerous goods. The legislation aims to ensure the safe transportation of hazardous substances to preserve to public and environmental safety.
The Canadian Environmental Protection Act
The Canadian Environmental Protection Act regulates emission levels for trucks and other vehicles. Emission level restrictions mirror standards set by the US Environmental Protection Agency. For example, government agencies in both Canada and the United States have both imposed federal restrictions on lead-based gasoline, offered various subsidies to gasoline companies that use ethanol-based fuel and set strict standards for nitrogen oxide and sulphur emissions.
Driver safety standards
Hours of service regulations aim to prevent driver fatigue by limiting driving and on-duty hours. In Canada, truck drivers can drive up to 13 hours per day and be on duty for 70 hours over seven days. In contrast, US regulations allow 11 continuous driving hours within a 14-hour window and 60 on-duty hours over seven days. These differences create challenges for cross-border trucking operations.

What assistance is available to this industry?
Government Provincial incentives
Provinces in Canada offer various incentives including tax credits, low-interest loans and rebates for local specialized freight truckers. These programs encourage truckers to upgrade to environmentally friendly and fuel-efficient vehicles, aiming to reduce emissions and promote sustainability within the sector. Provincial assistance helps lower operational costs and makes it financially feasible for trucking companies to invest in newer, more efficient vehicles, helping offset lackluster federal support.
Non-government Trade associations
Companies receive help from the Canadian Trucking Alliance, a federation of provincial trucking associations that represents its members. The alliance includes the British Columbia Trucking Association, the Alberta Motor Transport Association, the Saskatchewan Trucking Association, the Ontario Trucking Association, the Atlantic Provinces Trucking Association, the Quebec Trucking Association and the Manitoba Trucking Association. The alliance helps local specialized freight truckers stay up-to-date with the latest trends. It also lobbies governments and helps set industry standards.

What trends impact industry costs?
Profit thins amid input cost volatility
•
Oil price volatility pressured profit as carriers were forced to absorb rising prices despite implementing surcharges to mitigate the spike. Small operating fleets prevent many carriers from seeking bulk purchase discounts, exacerbating the impact of rising costs and pressuring profit.
•
Growing price competition has forced some carriers to lower prices. Even so, just-in-time (JIT) inventory management has led to more frequent, smaller shipments, increasing freight turnover and leading to higher revenues.
•
Local specialized freight trucking companies are often more profitable than companies transporting standardized cargo. Truckers are insulated from more intense price competition since the specialized equipment and training required make it harder for new companies to join the industry compared to other trucking
industries.
•
The specialized nature of the services provided insulated the industry from the oversaturation experienced in the general freight trucking businesses across the truckload and less-than-truckload distribution. The niche specializations reduced volatility and allowed companies to preserve profit despite slight deterioration due to rising expenses.
The specialized nature of equipment drives depreciation expense
•
Depreciation expense varies by the number and type of vehicles operating in a company's fleet. Trucks and trailers are the primary depreciable assets for carriers. The mileage, vehicle maintenance procedures and the technology that the trucks are equipped with influence the depreciation rate to which the carriers are exposed to.
Local Specialized Freight Trucking in Canada
•
The industry's specialized nature often causes depreciation to be higher than for other trucking industries. Tankers and pneumatic trailers have higher capital costs. According to SafeRack estimates, a sand and clay-carrying trailer can cost up to $110,000, with special vacuum trailers costing upwards of $140,000. For comparison, a standard dry van trailer tends to cost between $35,000 and $40,000.
•
Technological innovations like improved fuel efficiency, technology or safety features tend to drive down the salvage value of older truck models, causing the depreciation expense to hasten. The impact is particularly pronounced on smaller carriers without the capital to upgrade their fleets, forcing them to operate outdated truck models for a longer duration than large enterprises.
Volatile fuel costs drive purchasing expenses
•
Fuel and truck costs make up the vast majority of truckers' purchases. When fuel prices climb, truckers look to pass them on to clients through fuel surcharges. Heightened competition can reduce a carrier's ability to levy surcharges, but larger trucking companies with strong reputations often have greater success when passing costs downstream because of their capacity to handle large contracts, which makes vendors more willing to accept transportation rates.
•
Businesses must invest in the newest technology to remain competitive, particularly in dry bulk transport that is prone to contamination if the trailer isn't cleaned properly between shipments. Integrating more sensors and technology into trucks drives efficiency, but it also comes with a higher price tag for the vehicles, raising purchase costs and boosting the barriers to entry for small enterprises.
•
Volatile crude oil prices have slammed most local specialized truckers. While most companies successfully passed costs onto buyers, purchase costs have remained elevated and profit depressed. Many companies will also subcontract nonemployers and smaller companies to deliver if they don't have the necessary trucks or serve a specific geographic area. This structure enables larger companies to retain flexibility without investing in new equipment and trucks.
Manual labour is critical to daily operations
•
Most employees are truck drivers. Canada has fewer driver regulations related to hours of service compared with companies in the US, allowing Canadian localized special freight truckers to spend less on wages. The remaining labourers are involved in freight handling, warehousing and administration.
•
Larger companies have more effective itinerary and inventory management, often maximizing technology and labour-saving machinery use. Similarly, larger companies will subcontract part-time drivers and nonemployers, lowering wage costs.
•
Labour costs are more difficult to manage for nonemployers because owner-operators can only increase operating capacity and meet elevated demand by working longer hours or charging higher prices.
